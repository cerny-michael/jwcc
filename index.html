    How the three figures are built

    Journals impose word limits but define words differently, and most instructions are incomplete. This page separates the two problems: how to count a word, and what to count.

    Counting a word

    A word is a whitespace-delimited token containing at least one letter or digit — the convention Microsoft Word and LibreOffice implement, and the one journal limits assume, since that is the number authors read off their own status bar. So hyphenated compounds (state-building) count once, comma-grouped numbers (1,254) count once, em and en dashes separate words, and stray punctuation counts for nothing. In a .docx, tracked deletions are dropped and tracked insertions kept, so the figure is the document with all changes accepted; reviewer comments and reference-manager field codes are never counted.

    What to count

    The document is split into ten components — front matter, abstract, main text, notes, captions, table contents, references, in-document appendices, acknowledgements and display equations. Each journal's published rule marks every component as counted, not counted, or unaddressed. From that:

    • Lower bound — only components the journal explicitly counts. This is the most favourable defensible reading of the instructions.
    • Upper bound — every component the journal does not explicitly exclude. This is what a strict editor could hold you to.
    • Best estimate — the journal's explicit rules, with anything it leaves unaddressed resolved by the standard scope below.

    When the bounds are far apart, the journal's instructions are ambiguous, not the document.

    The standard scope

    For a document with no journal selected, and to resolve gaps in a journal's rule, this page uses the scope that is modal across the journals in the dataset: main text, footnotes and endnotes, and table and figure captions count; front matter, abstract and keywords, text inside tables, the reference list, in-document appendices, acknowledgements and display equations do not. It is a manuscript-text convention rather than a whole-file convention, and it matches how most of the journals here that do specify their rules in full define the count. You can override any row in the breakdown table.

    Where the journal data comes from

    Always check the journal's own instructions before submitting. A limit can change the week after a dataset is compiled, and several of the entries here are flagged as unverified, incomplete or internally contradictory.
