      How the Numbers are Calculated

      Although most journals impose word limits, they define a word differently, and their instructions are often incomplete. Word processors do not agree either: the same file can return different totals in Microsoft Word, Google Docs, LibreOffice, and Pages, depending on how each treats dashes, footnotes, and text boxes. In this section, I explain how this page separates these two problems, namely how to count a word and what to count.

      Counting a Word

      I define a word as any whitespace-delimited token that contains at least one letter or digit. This is the convention that word processors such as Microsoft Word and LibreOffice broadly follow and, importantly, the one most journal limits assume. Hyphenated compounds such as state-building and comma-grouped numbers such as 1,254 therefore count once, whereas stray punctuation counts for nothing. I also treat em and en dashes as separators, as LibreOffice does by default. In a .docx, I drop tracked deletions and keep tracked insertions, so that the count reflects the document with all changes accepted. Reviewer comments and reference-manager field codes are never counted, and a text box, which Word stores twice, is counted only once.

      Finding the Parts

      Since journals count some parts of a manuscript and not others, I split each document into ten components: front matter, abstract, main text, notes, captions, table contents, references, in-document appendices, acknowledgements, and display equations. To do so, I locate sections by their headings, including Word heading styles, short lines set entirely in bold, and lines that name a section, such as ‘Introduction’ or ‘Data and Methods’. In a .docx, a page break inserted after the abstract also marks its end. To check where each boundary fell, open ‘How the document was split’ beneath the result.

      What to Count

      Once a journal is chosen, its published rule marks each component as counted, not counted, or unaddressed. From these rules, I calculate three figures:

      • Lower bound: only the components the journal explicitly counts. This is the most favorable defensible reading of its instructions.
      • Upper bound: every component the journal does not explicitly exclude. This is the figure a strict editor could hold an author to.
      • Best estimate: the journal's explicit rules, with anything it leaves unaddressed resolved by the standard scope described below.

      When the two bounds are far apart, in other words, it is the journal's instructions that are ambiguous rather than the document. Choosing your own rules instead marks every component as either counted or not, which yields a single count.

      The Standard Scope

      Before a journal is chosen, and to fill any gaps in its rule, I count the text of the manuscript proper. More specifically, main text, footnotes and endnotes, and table and figure captions count, whereas front matter, the abstract and keywords, text inside tables, the reference list, in-document appendices, acknowledgements, and display equations do not. Among the journals that address them, more count the reference list, table text, and the abstract than exclude them. Where a journal is silent on these parts, they therefore remain in the upper bound, which is the figure to watch when expecting a strict reading. Any row can also be overridden in the breakdown table.

      Where the Journal Data Comes From

      Journal limits and counting criteria were last updated on 16 October 2026. Unless otherwise noted, each entry refers to research articles. Where an entry is marked ‘Not verified’, I could not confirm the journal's policy. Where it is marked ‘Not stated’, the journal's instructions give no limit, but this does not mean that it accepts manuscripts of any length. Where a journal names a length to aim for rather than a cap, I measure the result against that figure as a guideline.

      Since a limit can change after it is recorded, and several entries here are flagged as unverified, incomplete, or internally contradictory, always check the journal's own instructions before submitting.
